# Architecture

patient-caller is a small Python program that phones a clinic's AI receptionist and plays a patient. Each call follows a scenario from `scenarios/*.yaml`: who the patient is, what they want in what order, and when they're done. A LiveKit Agents worker runs on my machine. `py -m caller run` creates a LiveKit room and dispatches the worker into it. The worker dials out through a LiveKit outbound SIP trunk, which hands the call to a Twilio Elastic SIP trunk, which places the real phone call from my Twilio number. Once the receptionist picks up, their audio arrives in the room as one more participant, and the patient's voice goes back the same way. Hanging up just means deleting the room, which drops the phone leg. The number we dial is a constant in `config.py` and is checked again right before every dial, so no scenario file or env var can send a call anywhere else. The phone setup was the bumpiest part. Twilio rejected my first number as caller ID (errors 32204 and 21210) even though I'd bought it. Twilio's debugger showed LiveKit sending exactly the right number, so the number itself was stuck, and a fresh one fixed it.

It's a plain pipeline with three separate models and no speech-to-speech. Speech-to-text is Deepgram Nova-3, streaming. AssemblyAI's streaming model was the alternative. I went with Deepgram because it returns final transcripts fast, keeps filler words (which the turn detector uses), and has the most worn-in LiveKit plugin. Phone audio is 8 kHz narrowband. The room hands it to the pipeline once, resampled to 16 kHz, which Deepgram and Silero VAD both accept natively, so nothing downstream converts it again. I first sent Deepgram 8 kHz, but that added a second resampler, and on Windows LiveKit's audio library crashed with an assertion inside that resampler. The LLM is gpt-4.1-mini. A patient doesn't need deep reasoning. It needs to answer quickly and stay on its goals, and a small model that doesn't reason first has a short time to first token. Claude Haiku would have done the job too; OpenAI was cheaper per token and the LiveKit path is well trodden. Text-to-speech is Cartesia Sonic-3. In my test calls it produced its first audio in about 0.1 to 0.2 seconds, and its voices that sound like people on a phone rather than narrators. ElevenLabs Flash was the alternative. It sounds great, but it costs more per character and I didn't need its extra range. The persona prompt stays around 250 words, keeps the patient to one or two short sentences per turn, and the bot never says anything until the receptionist has greeted it.

Turn-taking is where phone bots usually fall apart. VAD-only endpointing ends the turn after a fixed stretch of silence, and a receptionist pauses mid-sentence all the time, between two halves of a sentence or while looking something up. A VAD-only bot jumps into those pauses and talks over them. Here, Silero VAD only answers "is someone speaking right now", and LiveKit's English turn-detector model reads the words so far and judges whether the sentence is finished. If it sounds finished, the patient answers after half a second of silence. If not, it waits up to three seconds. In the second test call the receptionist paused for about five seconds between "There are several morning openings this Thursday, October fifteenth" and the doctor's name, and the patient correctly waited. Interruptions are on: if the receptionist starts talking over the patient, the patient stops, like a person would. The one exception is the barge-in scenario, which deliberately cuts the receptionist off once to correct a date of birth, and that line can't be interrupted. Hanging up goes through an `end_call` tool. The patient says goodbye, we wait for that to finish playing and for the receptionist to go quiet, then delete the room. In the first call gpt-4.1-mini wrote "functions.end_call()" as text and the voice read it out, so the LLM stream now strips that pattern and treats it as a real hang-up (see ITERATIONS.md).

For latency, preemptive generation is on, so the LLM starts on the receptionist's words while the turn detector is still deciding. TTS streams, so audio starts with the first words instead of the whole reply. The prompt is short, and the only tool is `end_call`, which is never on the path of a normal reply. The worker keeps one process warm with the VAD already loaded. Every reply's gap, meaning the receptionist's last word to the patient's first audio, is logged in `transcript.json`, and `py -m caller review` prints the median across all calls. Over about a hundred replies in my test calls the median was 1.2 seconds, and the slowest was 4.9. For recording, I looked at LiveKit Egress first, but it needs an S3 or R2 bucket plus a download step. LiveKit Agents 1.8 already records each session itself as a stereo OGG, with the clinic on the left channel and the patient on the right, both on one timeline. The worker copies that file into the call's folder when the call ends. The session only starts once the clinic answers, so there's no ringing at the front of the recording, and the timestamps in `transcript.txt` match the audio.
